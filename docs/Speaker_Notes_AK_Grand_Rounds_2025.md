## SLIDE 4: What is ctDNA?

**Speaking point:**
- Circulating tumor DNA, or ctDNA, is tumor-derived DNA fragments that are released into the bloodstream by cancer cells.
- These fragments can be detected through liquid biopsy - a simple blood draw - rather than requiring tissue biopsy.
- The DNA fragments are short, typically 150-200 base pairs, and represent a small fraction of total cell-free DNA in circulation.
- The concept has been around for decades, but recent advances in sequencing technology have made it clinically feasible.

**Key points to emphasize:**
- Non-invasive compared to tissue biopsy
- Real-time monitoring capability
- Can detect disease earlier than imaging

**Transition:**
- There are two main platforms for ctDNA testing, and it's important to understand the difference.

---

## SLIDE 5: ctDNA Testing Platforms

**Speaking point:**
- There are two main approaches to ctDNA detection:
  - **Tumor-informed approaches** (like Signatera): Require sequencing of the primary tumor first, then creating a personalized panel. This is more sensitive and specific but requires tumor tissue.
  - **Tumor-agnostic approaches**: Use fixed panels of common mutations. More convenient but generally less sensitive.
- For bladder cancer, most of the evidence we'll discuss today uses tumor-informed approaches, specifically Signatera.
- The tumor-informed approach allows detection of very low levels of ctDNA, which is critical for monitoring in early-stage disease.

**Transition:**
- Now, what are the clinical applications of ctDNA testing?

---

## SLIDE 6: Clinical Applications of ctDNA Testing

**Speaking point:**
- ctDNA testing has several potential applications across the cancer care continuum:
  - **Diagnosis and screening**: Detecting cancer earlier
  - **Prognosis**: Identifying patients at high or low risk
  - **Monitoring treatment response**: Real-time assessment of therapy effectiveness
  - **Residual disease detection**: Identifying minimal residual disease after surgery
  - **Recurrence monitoring**: Detecting recurrence earlier than imaging
- In bladder cancer specifically, we're seeing strong evidence in multiple settings, which we'll explore today.

## SLIDE 8: NIAGRA Trial: Key Findings

**Speaking point:**
- The NIAGRA trial, presented by Powles and colleagues at ASCO 2025, gave us important insights about baseline ctDNA in the neoadjuvant setting.
- **Key finding #1**: Baseline ctDNA is **prognostic** - it tells us who is at higher risk for poor outcomes.
- **But critically**: Baseline ctDNA is **NOT yet predictive** - we cannot use it to select which patients should or shouldn't get neoadjuvant therapy.
- **Dynamic changes during therapy may be informative** - this suggests monitoring ctDNA during neoadjuvant treatment could be valuable, but we need more data.
- **Bottom line**: We cannot use baseline ctDNA to omit neoadjuvant therapy. All eligible patients should still receive standard neoadjuvant chemotherapy.

**Emphasis:**
- This is a crucial clinical point: ctDNA is prognostic but not predictive in this setting
- We cannot use it to change standard of care treatment decisions

**Transition:**
- So what are the unanswered questions in neoadjuvant therapy?

---

## SLIDE 9: Unanswered Questions in Neoadjuvant Therapy

**Speaking point:**
- This is where ongoing research is focused. There's a trial called NEO-BLAST that's exploring a critical question:
- Can we use ctDNA combined with bladder MRI and TURBT to accurately identify patients who achieve a clinical complete response to neoadjuvant therapy?
- And if so, is active surveillance non-inferior to standard definitive therapy - which is radical cystectomy?
- This is the "bladder preservation" question - can we spare some patients from cystectomy if they achieve a complete response?
- This trial, led by St-Laurent and colleagues, was presented at AUA 2025 and is currently under investigation.
- If successful, this could be practice-changing, but we need the trial results first.

**Key point:**
- This represents a paradigm shift - using ctDNA to potentially avoid surgery in complete responders
- But we need prospective trial data before this becomes standard of care

**Transition:**

## SLIDE 10: Pre-cystectomy ctDNA Threshold Identifies Patients at High Risk for Post-Operative MRD

**Speaking point:**
- This is preliminary data from our own work, analyzing patients who underwent cystectomy.
- **Cohort**: We studied 59 evaluable patients at the 3-month post-cystectomy landmark.
- **Co-Primary Endpoint 1 - Association**: Pre-cystectomy ctDNA significantly predicts post-cystectomy molecular residual disease, or MRD.
- The odds ratio is 1.89 per 10-fold increase in pre-cystectomy ctDNA - this is a strong, statistically significant association with a p-value less than 0.001.
- **Co-Primary Endpoint 2 - Threshold Performance**: We identified a threshold of 0.1 MTM/mL, which is bootstrap-validated.
- At this threshold, sensitivity is approximately 82% - meaning we catch 82% of patients who will have persistent MRD.
- Specificity is 68%, positive predictive value is 60%, and negative predictive value is 86%.
- **Conclusion**: Pre-cystectomy ctDNA above 0.1 MTM/mL reliably identifies patients at high risk for persistent MRD after surgery.

**Clinical implications:**
- This is for **risk stratification** - identifying who is at high risk
- The threshold serves to inform monitoring intensity and adjuvant therapy considerations
- It's not a decision tool for surgery vs. additional therapy - all patients still proceed to cystectomy
- But it helps us identify patients who may warrant earlier or more intensive adjuvant therapy

## SLIDE 12: ctDNA Identifies Patients Who Benefit From ICI Adjuvant Therapy

**Speaking point:**
- This data from Powles and colleagues in European Urology Oncology 2022 was one of the first major findings in adjuvant ctDNA.
- They showed that **ctDNA-positive patients** after cystectomy benefit significantly from adjuvant immunotherapy with checkpoint inhibitors.
- The hazard ratio is 0.59 with a 95% confidence interval of 0.42 to 0.83.
- This means ctDNA-positive patients who received adjuvant ICI had about a 40% reduction in the risk of disease recurrence or death compared to those who didn't receive it.
- This is a substantial benefit and was the first strong signal that ctDNA could guide adjuvant therapy decisions.

**Key point:**
- ctDNA-positive status identifies patients who benefit from adjuvant ICI
- This is predictive, not just prognostic

**Transition:**
- This finding has been replicated in larger trials.

---

## SLIDE 13: CheckMate 274: Replication of Findings

**Speaking point:**
- CheckMate 274 was a landmark trial that randomized high-risk patients after cystectomy to adjuvant nivolumab or placebo.
- The updated results, presented by Galsky and colleagues at ESMO 2025, now include ctDNA analysis.
- The key finding: Adjuvant immunotherapy with nivolumab improves disease-free survival specifically for ctDNA-positive patients.
- High-risk was defined as ≥ypT2/N+ or ≥pT3/N+ after radical cystectomy.
- This confirms the earlier findings - ctDNA-positive patients benefit from adjuvant ICI, and this is now supported by randomized trial data.

**Emphasis:**
- This is level 1 evidence from a randomized trial
- ctDNA status is identifying the population that benefits from adjuvant ICI

**Transition:**
- But we've now moved to a more sophisticated approach - using ctDNA to actually guide treatment decisions.

---

## SLIDE 14: IMvigor011: First Prospective ctDNA-Guided Trial

**Speaking point:**
- IMvigor011, published by Powles and colleagues in the New England Journal of Medicine in 2025, was a game-changer.
- This was the **first prospective, ctDNA-guided trial** in bladder cancer.
- The design: Patients after cystectomy had ctDNA testing. Only ctDNA-positive patients were randomized to adjuvant atezolizumab or surveillance.
- This is different from CheckMate 274, where all high-risk patients were enrolled regardless of ctDNA status.
- IMvigor011 showed that ctDNA-guided treatment - only treating ctDNA-positive patients - is effective.
- This represents a more personalized, biomarker-driven approach to adjuvant therapy.

**Key innovation:**
- Using ctDNA to **select** patients for treatment, not just to identify who benefits among treated patients
- This is the future direction - biomarker-guided treatment selection

**Transition:**
- But there are still unanswered questions in the adjuvant setting.

---

## SLIDE 15: Unanswered Questions in Adjuvant Therapy

**Speaking point:**
- As we develop ctDNA-guided approaches, two key questions emerge, adapted from Hemenway and colleagues' work in the ASCO Educational Book 2024.
- **De-escalation**: Can we safely watch-and-wait if ctDNA is negative? This is the question of whether ctDNA-negative patients truly have low enough risk that we can avoid adjuvant therapy.
- **Treatment escalation**: Should ctDNA-positive patients get dual immunotherapy - perhaps combining checkpoint inhibitors or adding other agents? This is exploring whether we can do better than single-agent ICI for high-risk ctDNA-positive patients.
- These are active areas of research, and we'll need prospective trials to answer them.

## SLIDE 17: ctDNA Identifies Low-Risk Patients After Surgery

**Speaking point:**
- Data from IMvigor011, analyzed by Eraky and colleagues and published in European Urology Oncology 2025, showed remarkable findings in surveillance.
- **Persistently ctDNA-negative patients** had excellent outcomes: 95% disease-free survival at 12 months.
- In contrast, patients whose ctDNA converted from negative to positive had much worse outcomes: only about 40% disease-free survival at 12 months.
- Additionally, ctDNA detected recurrence a median of **90 days earlier** than imaging - this is a substantial lead time that could allow for earlier intervention.

**Clinical implications:**
- ctDNA-negative status identifies patients with excellent prognosis who may need less intensive surveillance
- ctDNA conversion from negative to positive is a strong signal of recurrence
- The lead time advantage suggests ctDNA could improve outcomes through earlier detection

**Transition:**
- But can we actually use ctDNA to guide surveillance?

## SLIDE 21: Quantitative Baseline ctDNA is Prognostic

**Speaking point:**
- Prior meta-analysis by Liu and colleagues showed that qualitative baseline ctDNA - just positive or negative - is associated with worse overall survival, with a hazard ratio of 2.06.
- But we asked: does the **quantity** of ctDNA matter? Does higher ctDNA levels predict worse outcomes?
- We retrospectively analyzed 38 patients with metastatic bladder cancer who had baseline ctDNA measured within 21 days before starting a new line of systemic therapy - this gave us 48 observations.
- **Preliminary finding**: Quantitative baseline ctDNA is prognostic - higher levels are associated with worse overall survival.
- This is shown in the figure on the right, where higher log-transformed ctDNA levels are associated with increased hazard ratios - meaning increased risk of death.

**Clinical implication:**
- Not just whether ctDNA is positive, but how much matters
- Quantitative ctDNA levels provide more granular prognostic information

**Transition:**
- But perhaps more importantly, can ctDNA response predict outcomes?

---

## SLIDE 22: ctDNA Clearance as a Surrogate Endpoint

**Speaking point:**
- Prior work by Powles and colleagues in Nature Medicine 2024 showed that ctDNA reduction - specifically C2/C1 reduction below the median during ICI monotherapy - is associated with better overall survival.
- Our preliminary work asks: does ctDNA **clearance** - going from positive to negative - predict outcomes?
- We found that ctDNA clearance between 6-24 weeks from starting therapy shows significantly improved overall survival, with a p-value of 0.01 after adjusting for line of therapy.
- This suggests that ctDNA clearance could serve as a surrogate endpoint - an early marker of treatment effectiveness.

**Clinical implications:**
- ctDNA clearance may allow us to assess treatment response earlier than imaging
- This could enable earlier treatment switches or dose adjustments
- But we need more validation before using ctDNA clearance as a formal surrogate endpoint

**Transition:**
- This raises the question: how do we standardize ctDNA response assessment?

---

## SLIDE 24: RECIST Criteria for ctDNA Response

**Speaking point:**
- Gouda and colleagues in Annals of Oncology 2023 proposed RECIST-like criteria for ctDNA response.
- The concept is similar to RECIST: we need standardized definitions of complete response, partial response, stable disease, and progressive disease based on ctDNA levels.
- However, unlike imaging, ctDNA can go to zero - true complete response is achievable.
- This is important for standardization across trials and clinical practice.

**Transition:**
- But what's the best way to measure ctDNA response?

---

## SLIDE 25: Qualitative ctDNA Response Criteria is Non-inferior to Quantitative and ML Models

**Speaking point:**
- This is preliminary data from our work asking: do we need complex quantitative or machine learning approaches, or is simple qualitative assessment - positive or negative - sufficient?
- We compared three approaches:
  - **Quantitative**: Using precise ctDNA levels and thresholds
  - **Qualitative**: Simple positive/negative assessment
  - **Machine learning**: Sophisticated models incorporating multiple features
- **Finding**: Qualitative ctDNA response criteria is non-inferior to both quantitative and machine learning approaches.
- This is important because qualitative assessment is simpler, more reproducible, and more clinically practical.
- Sometimes the simple answer is the right answer.

**Clinical implication:**
- We don't necessarily need complex models - simple positive/negative ctDNA assessment may be sufficient for response evaluation
- This makes ctDNA more accessible and practical for clinical use
